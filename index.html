ABOUT

A software engineer who kept asking why the annotators disagreed

I'm a Software Engineer at Bdjobs.com Limited, where I spend most days on ASP.NET Core and Angular, and lately on an AI-based matching score that ranks candidate profiles against job postings. Before that I interned at Dhaka Bank PLC, building a face-recognition attendance system, which was a fairly ordinary way into the industry.

The research side started with my undergraduate thesis. My team collected thousands of social media posts from cancer patients and their caregivers and had people label the emotions in them. What stuck with me wasn't the model results so much as how often the human annotators disagreed with each other on posts that were genuinely ambiguous. Most of what I'd read treated that disagreement as noise to average away. It felt more like the actual signal worth paying attention to.

That question, of when and why models fail to notice what humans clearly disagree about, is what pulled me toward NLP research alongside my day job. Right now I'm working on a workshop paper that tests whether showing a model's confidence score changes how annotators anchor on its suggestion, and I'd like to eventually take these questions into low-resource and code-mixed Bengali text, where very little of this has been studied.

I studied Computer Science and Engineering at BRAC University. Outside of work, I'm usually somewhere between a research paper and a dataset I'm trying to clean up.

RESEARCH FOCUS

Three questions I keep returning to

That annotators disagree is already well documented, that part isn't new. What I want to know is narrower: whether a model's own confidence score actually tracks that disagreement, whether calibrated uncertainty holds up when the stakes are health-related, and whether any of it survives the jump to low-resource Bengali text.

CONFIDENCE VS. CONSENSUS

Does model confidence track genuine human uncertainty?

Disagreement itself is well studied. Less clear is whether a model's confidence score changes how annotators anchor on its suggested label, especially on the items people disagree about most. A pilot study of mine tests this directly.

PUBLICATIONS

Peer-reviewed work

A Qualitative Study of the Mental Health Experiences of Cancer Patients with the Lowest Survival Rate and Their Caregivers

First author
IEEE ICCIT 2023 · DOI 10.1109/ICCIT60459.2023.10441282

Built a 10,087-post dataset drawn from cancer-related social media discussion, with 2,373 posts manually annotated for emotion. Inter-annotator agreement landed at a Cohen's Kappa of 0.40 to 0.60, itself a signal of how genuinely ambiguous emotional language can be in a health context. Compared GRU, few-shot SBERT, and SVM baselines.

GRU (TF-IDF + FastText): 75% accuracy SBERT few-shot: F1 0.82, 71% accuracy SVM baseline: 62% accuracy Cohen's Kappa: 0.40–0.60

Mitigating Online Harassment: Machine Learning Approaches for Hate Speech Detection in Transliterated Bengali Comments

Co-author
IEEE ICCIT 2023 · DOI 10.1109/ICCIT60459.2023.10441244

Contributed to the first public corpus of transliterated Bengali hate speech, built from 5,000 Facebook comments, including a custom preprocessing pipeline since no standard tokenizer or stopword list existed for the transliterated form. Benchmarked seven classifiers across TF-IDF and bag-of-words representations.

Binary SVM + TF-IDF: F1 0.741 Binary Bi-LSTM: F1 0.422 Multiclass SVM: F1 0.578
